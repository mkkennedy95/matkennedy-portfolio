The Situation

When I joined Matos Law Firm in 2019, the brand existed mostly in theory. Social media accounts were live but dormant. Passwords were scattered across Post-it notes. There was no content strategy, no intake tracking, no systems of any kind. The previous marketing approach had been relationship-based — showing up at events, connecting with chiropractors, building a name in the community. It was working well enough to average around 10 cases per month, but nothing had been documented, measured, or scaled.

My first job was to bring the brand to life. I built a full content operation from scratch — planning, writing, filming, editing, and posting everything myself across every platform, multiple times a day. I got the owner onto TV shows, podcasts, speaking engagements, and radio spots, and mined every appearance for content. I designed the visual identity. I wrote the copy. I set up the tracking. For the first time, the firm had a daily presence and a real brand voice.

Over the next few years, the firm grew. We brought in paid advertising vendors — Facebook ads, Google search, SEO. I took on the role of directing those relationships while continuing to run all content in-house. By end of 2023, we were averaging [TK] cases per month and spending $25,000/month on marketing.

Then everything changed at once.

The Breaking Point

Going into 2024, two of the firm's top attorneys resigned. The office manager — the owner's right hand — left at the same time. The owner herself was dealing with serious health issues. Content production ground to a halt. I spent most of that year recycling old footage, writing, and quietly helping hold the operations of the business together — building hiring systems, tracking resumes, helping run interviews — because nobody else was doing it.

Then, at the end of 2024, the owner called me in and told me the marketing budget was being cut from $25,000 a month to $5,000. Immediately. The paid ads vendors were gone. The SEO company was gone. She would be largely unavailable for content.

I had no subject to film, no vendors, almost no budget, and a boss who couldn't show up. I had to figure out what we actually had going for us.

I did something I hadn't done before: I sat down, loaded all of our business metrics into AI, and asked it to help me find our real strengths. The answer was clear. Despite everything — despite years of paid ads and content and vendor relationships — most of our clients were still coming from word-of-mouth referrals. The community was our actual engine. We just hadn't been treating it that way.

The Insight

When I looked at who was actually sending us clients, one pattern kept emerging: Latina women. Specifically, high-achieving Latina women — business owners, professionals, connectors. Latino men, culturally, are often reluctant to ask for help. Their wives make the call. And those same women would tell their clients, their friends, their networks about us.

Personal injury law is one of those industries where most people have no idea who to call when something goes wrong. A trusted referral from someone they respect changes everything. If we could get the right Latina women to know and trust Matos Law Firm, they would do the marketing for us.

We already had something that could become that engine. For years, the firm had been hosting a small monthly event called Women and Wine — a casual happy hour averaging about 25–30 attendees. Nobody had treated it as a serious brand asset. I decided to change that.

What We Built

Arriba Las Mujeres

I rebranded the event from the ground up. New name. New identity. New ethos. I designed a logo centered around a red heel — an image that became the visual signature of everything we do. I built out the digital presence: a Facebook group, an Instagram page, an Eventbrite presence.

But the bigger shift was in what the event was actually for. We stopped trying to make it feel like a law firm marketing event and started building it around a mission: creating a room where Latina women who wanted more for themselves could find the people and opportunities to make that happen. The speeches are in Spanish. The music is Bad Bunny. The lights are red. We brought in a DJ, a photo booth, a red carpet, themed cocktails, and a different 'wow' item at every event — something guests would photograph and post.

On event nights, I move through the room with an iPhone, capturing everything. That footage becomes a trailer for the next event — opening with a direct hook: 'This is a free Latina networking event in Denver' — and showing women dancing, laughing, connecting. Those trailers now get hundreds of organic shares every month. We cap RSVPs at 150. We sell out every time, days in advance, with zero paid promotion.

Women drive from Wyoming to attend. Restaurants and business owners in Denver reach out asking to host us or get involved. People have gotten jobs at these events. Businesses have been started. The community now has 1,300+ members and draws 120 attendees per month — roughly 80 of them first-timers.

The moment it clicked was when I stopped trying to be clever and just invited people into the room. Simplicity was the strategy.

Conexión Colorado Newsletter

The event needed somewhere to lead. I built a weekly email newsletter — Conexión Colorado — focused on what's happening in Denver's Latina community. The goal of every event is simple: get attendees onto the email list. From there, we provide value on a weekly basis and stay top of mind without spending anything.

The newsletter now sits at a 25% average open rate — well above industry standard — and has become one of the firm's most consistent marketing touchpoints.

The Referral System

If referrals were our real engine, I wanted to build a real system around them. I mapped the full client journey and created structured touchpoints: review request sequences, email nurture flows, and source tracking so we could finally see exactly where every case was coming from.

The result is an 83% case conversion rate. When someone calls Matos Law Firm today, they almost always become a client — because the people calling already trust us before they pick up the phone.

The Results

By the end of 2025 — operating at 20% of our previous marketing budget, with no paid ads, no outside vendors, and a largely unavailable owner — Matos Law Firm was averaging 14.6 cases signed per month.

That number is [TK — insert comparison to peak vendor-supported performance]. We didn't just survive the budget cut. We built something more sustainable than what existed before it.

What the numbers don't show: the firm is now better known in Denver's Latina community than it has ever been. We have a community that markets itself. We have an email list that actually gets opened. We have a referral system that converts. And we have a monthly event that sells out — without spending a dollar to promote it.

What This Taught Me

The best marketing insight I got at Matos Law Firm didn't come from a strategy deck or a vendor recommendation. It came from looking honestly at what was actually working — and then going all in on that instead of what I thought marketing was supposed to look like.

Constraints don't kill good ideas. They reveal which ones are actually worth having.
